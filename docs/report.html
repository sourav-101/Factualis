Abstract Compliance staff need answers they can trace to a specific clause, and a fluent answer drawn from a model's general knowledge is worse than no answer at all. We present Factualis, a question-answering system for a fictional Indian bank that answers only from the bank's own policy library, quotes the governing clause verbatim and abstains when the policy is silent. Every quote and figure in an answer is checked against the source text by a deterministic verifier. The library combines three public regulations with three internal procedures whose thresholds were chosen to differ from common practice, so that answers from general knowledge are detectably wrong. Training data was generated with CuratorKIT using a 7B teacher served by SGLang and filtered word for word; Qwen2.5-3B was then fine-tuned with SFT and DPO using AlignTune on a single T4, and every evaluation was graded with AuditKIT on 94 hand-verified questions. Structured decoding in SGLang raised the share of checkable answers from 0% to 100%, and RadixAttention reduced time to first token by a factor of 9.3. The fine-tuned model refuses answerable questions significantly less often than the base model (17.6% against 28.4%, p = 0.02) and makes about half as many unsupported claims per answer, while the two tie on the strictest combined measure. We also report a failure in our first training run and trace it to the answer specification.
Keywords: grounded generation, hallucination, structured decoding, retrieval, preference optimisation, banking compliance

1Introduction

Problem Statement 3 asks for a model adapted to an organisation's documents that answers only from those documents, says so when the answer is not there, and makes measurably fewer unsupported claims. We chose banking compliance because the cost of a wrong answer is easy to see. A branch officer who asks whether a Rs 9 lakh RTGS transfer from a new customer needs review should get the bank's own rule, not the US USD 10,000 reporting threshold or a five-year retention period that a general model is likely to produce.

Our starting point is that a claim which cannot be traced to a clause should not be shown as an answer. Factualis therefore asks the model for a fixed structured answer in which every rule and quotation is tied to a clause, enforces that structure during decoding, and verifies the result with a program rather than a second model (Figure 1). This makes grounding measurable for every single answer, which in turn lets us filter training data, evaluate models and decide what users see with the same checks.

The contributions of this work are a policy library and test set built so that hallucinated thresholds are detectable; a pipeline that uses all four track tools on one free T4; an evaluation of structured decoding, prefix caching, SFT and DPO on this task; and an analysis of a regression in our first training run, which we traced to the answer specification rather than the training method.

2Policy Library and Test Set

Factualis Bank is fictional. Its library contains three public Indian regulations and three internal procedures that we wrote for this project (Table 2). The internal procedures deliberately use thresholds that differ from common practice (Table 1), and the regulations contribute realistic length and cross-referencing. Two documents, the SEBI insider trading regulations and the Employee Personal Dealing Policy, were excluded from training entirely so that we could measure grounding on unseen policy.

Documents are split into clause-level chunks so that a citation always resolves to a real clause number. Indian legal PDFs needed some care here: footnote markers attached to clause numbers, cross-references that wrap onto a new line, page watermarks and the Hindi half of bilingual gazettes all had to be handled. Retrieval is BM25 [6] over clauses, returning the top six.

The test set has 94 questions: 41 fully answerable, 20 near-miss traps that should be refused, 15 that are only partly answerable and 18 where two clauses give conflicting rules. We wrote 32 of them by hand and generated 62 with Gemini from the clause text, then reviewed each generated item. Every evidence quote was confirmed to appear verbatim in the corpus, every trap was checked against the closest clauses, 8 items were dropped as ambiguous and 8 were relabelled. Training questions were checked against the test set for exact and paraphrased overlap.

3Method

3.1Answer format and verification

Each answer is a JSON object with a short summary, a status, the extracted rules, verbatim citations and a list of the parts of the question that the policy does not cover (Figure 2). The status is one of fully grounded, partially grounded, regulatory conflict or not in policy. Decoding is constrained by the JSON schema through SGLang's XGrammar backend [2], so the output always parses.

The verifier then applies the checks in Table 3. A quote counts as grounded only if it appears in the retrieved clauses character for character; whitespace is ignored because extracted PDF text contains stray spaces inside words. Figures are compared digit group by digit group, and an answer to a trap question that claims to be grounded counts as a hallucination whatever its quotes say. The same checks filter the training data, produce the evaluation metrics and, in the deployed application, hold back any answer that fails them.

3.2Pipeline

Figure 3 shows the full pipeline. SGLang [1] serves both the teacher used for data generation and the deployed model, so the same server configuration and the same structured decoding are used throughout.

3.3Training data and fine-tuning

We fed 260 training clauses, internal procedures first and then a sample of the regulations, to two CuratorKIT pipelines driven by Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct in AWQ 4-bit [7, 8] on SGLang. The grounded pipeline produced 485 question-answer pairs from 243 clauses, 463 of which passed CuratorKIT's hallucination and reward filters. The adversarial pipeline produced 262 near-miss questions from 257 clauses.

The teacher then answered 600 questions (318 answerable, 218 traps and 64 partly answerable, drawn from CuratorKIT's output and our hand-written seeds) in the strict format, over the same six clauses the student would see. We kept an answer only if every quote and value was verbatim and the status matched the question type. Only 253 answers, 42%, survived (Figure 4). Even with its output format enforced, the teacher fabricated a quote or figure in 55 answers (9.2%) and answered 165 of the 218 traps.

For DPO, each rejected answer is a verified answer with exactly one fault introduced programmatically: a reworded quote (136 pairs), a refusal of an answerable question (121), a substituted threshold (75), a dropped condition (74) or an answer to a trap (49). Because the fault is constructed, every rejected answer is known to be wrong and each pair isolates a single failure mode.

Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct [7] was fine-tuned with SFT and then with DPO [3] using AlignTune (Table 4). The T4 has no bf16 support and cannot hold the SGLang memory pool and a training job together, so the server is stopped before training and the LoRA adapters are merged on the GPU in a separate process afterwards. DPO behaved as expected: by step 10 of 27 it preferred the verified answer in 92.5% of pairs, with a reward margin of 0.90.

4Experimental Setup

We compare five configurations that receive the same question, the same six retrieved clauses and the same instructions: A, the base model with prompting only; B, the base model with XGrammar; C, after SFT; D, after SFT and DPO; and E, the SFT and DPO model without XGrammar. All metrics come from the verifier in Section 3.1. The two held-out documents account for 15 test questions and are also reported separately. AuditKIT grades the saved outputs of each run, fingerprints the run with a hash over model, data, scorers and configuration, and compares two runs question by question. Significance is assessed with a paired sign-flip permutation test with 10,000 resamples.

5Results

5.1Initial evaluation

Structured decoding was the single most important component (Table 5). Without XGrammar, neither the base model nor the fine-tuned model produced one answer we could parse, while with it 92 to 100% of answers were valid. Prefix caching mattered for responsiveness: questions to the same bank share a long prefix of instructions and clauses, and reusing its cached attention state cut time to first token from 2,251 ms to 242 ms (Figure 6).

The fine-tuning results were mixed (Figure 5). DPO recovered much of what SFT had lost, reducing trap answers from 50% to 25%, held-out unsupported claims from 46.7% to 26.7% and raising abstention F1 from 0.56 to 0.70. However, the fine-tuned model did not improve on the base model inside the Factualis system, which remained the strongest configuration on hallucination and abstention.

5.2Error analysis

AuditKIT's question-by-question comparison showed that the regressions were significant (Figure 7), and the largest was not one our grounding metrics were designed to catch: the fine-tuned model left the summary empty in all 94 answers.

Two defects in the answer specification explain this. The schema asked for a compliance decision whose allowed values the instructions never defined, and every model, including the base model in all 94 answers, chose the single value mentioned in the refusal rule. The summary was optional and placed after the labels, and the base model already left it empty in 23 of 94 answers. The teacher's answers carried both defects, our filter checked what answers claimed but not what they omitted, and fine-tuning amplified the pattern. The fine-tuned model also adopted the teacher's longer answers (median 42 s against 15 s), which gave each answer more claims that could fail. Table 6 lists the corrections we made.

5.3Final evaluation

We re-evaluated the base and fine-tuned models on the same 94 questions with the corrected specification (AuditKIT run fingerprints 4e16c7a18cd5 and df64935d0b7c). Under the corrected specification the fine-tuned model's summaries returned, from 0% to 97.9%, and its share of answers with no unsupported claim rose from 67.0% to 77.7%, level with the base model.

Against the base model, the fine-tuned model refuses answerable questions significantly less often, makes about half as many unsupported claims per answer, quotes the policy verbatim more often and classifies questions on the held-out documents better (Figure 8, Table 7). It also answers three trap questions where the base model answers one, and its status labels agree less often with the content of the answer. On the strictest combined measure, an answer that is valid, has a summary, is internally consistent, carries the correct status and makes no unsupported claim, the two models tie at 41.5%. Given the small number of traps and held-out questions, only the differences in false refusals and status consistency are statistically reliable.

6Deployment

The application has a FastAPI backend and a single-page frontend and runs on a Colab T4 behind a public tunnel. Each answer shows the status, a plain-language summary, the extracted requirements marked as found or not found in the policy, and the verbatim quotes; a side panel opens the cited clause with the quotation highlighted. With Verified mode switched on, any answer containing a quote or figure that the verifier cannot find is withheld and marked for human review, following the flow in Figure 1. A switch for structured output lets a user see the raw text the model produces without XGrammar. Figures 9 to 14 show the application. Because the two models tie on the combined measure and the base model refuses traps more reliably, the live demonstration serves the base model.

7Limitations and Future Work

Retrieval is the main ceiling on answer quality: BM25 finds all of the gold evidence for 73% of answerable questions, and dense and hybrid retrieval did not improve on this for our corpus. Following clause cross-references and adding a reranker are the obvious next steps. The test set is small, with only 20 traps and 15 held-out items, so modest differences on those subsets are within noise, which is why we report paired significance tests throughout. Status accuracy on conflicting rules remains low, and more conflict examples in training, together with retrieval that pairs an internal procedure with the regulation it tightens, should help.

Most importantly, the training data was generated under the original answer specification. Relabelling it under the corrected specification, which the cached teacher answers make cheap, followed by a shorter SFT stage or DPO directly from the base model, is the experiment we would run next. Finally, the demonstration runs on a free Colab session; the same application could be deployed on a serverless GPU.

8Conclusion

Factualis shows that a 3B model running locally on a single T4 can give compliance answers whose every quote and figure is checked against the source. Most of the reliability comes from the system around the model: retrieval at the clause level, structured decoding in SGLang and a deterministic verifier that is used for data filtering, evaluation and deployment alike. Fine-tuning with SFT and DPO made the model refuse less and claim less without support, but it also inherited a weakness in our answer specification that only a usability-aware evaluation exposed. All code, data, predictions and AuditKIT reports needed to reproduce these results are in the repository.

Factualis Bank and its internal procedures are fictional and were written for this project. The RBI, PML and SEBI documents are public.
